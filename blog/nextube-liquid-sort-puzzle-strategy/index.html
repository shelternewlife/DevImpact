How to Solve Liquid Sort Puzzles: A Practical Strategy Guide for Nextube

Learn a clear strategy for liquid sort puzzles: read tube layers, protect empty space, uncover hidden colors and use Undo and Reset thoughtfully in Nextube.

DevImpact · 19 November 2026 · 2,500 words

Understand the puzzle before the first pour

A liquid sorting puzzle looks simple because its actions are simple: choose a tube, choose another tube, and watch a layer move. The thinking begins when several colors share the same containers and every available space has a purpose. A move can make the board look tidier while quietly removing the only route needed later. The most reliable way to solve a difficult level is therefore not to pour as quickly as possible. It is to understand the current arrangement, identify which moves preserve options, and use empty capacity deliberately. This guide explains a repeatable method for approaching color sorting puzzles, with examples grounded in the rules described for Nextube. The aim is to help you reason through a board rather than memorize a supposed universal sequence. Each level can differ, and a strategy that works on one arrangement may be unsuitable for another.

Nextube presents a neon-style liquid sorting challenge in which players tap a tube and then a destination tube. According to its published app description, a pour is allowed when the top colors match and the destination has room. The goal is to organize the liquid so that each tube contains one color. The app page also describes progressive levels, Undo and Reset controls, stars and coins, cosmetic tube and background options, and an offline-friendly experience. These are the features this guide relies on; it does not assume hidden mechanics, secret bonuses, a fixed number of moves, or an undocumented scoring formula. The first useful habit is to pause before making the opening move. Scan every tube from top to bottom and note which colors are visible, which colors are buried, and where the empty capacity sits. You do not need to write down the whole board on an easy level. On a crowded level, however, a quick mental inventory prevents a common mistake: treating the visible top layer as if it were the only color that matters. A tube with a red layer above blue and green is not simply a red tube. It is a sequence that may become accessible only after the upper layers move. Recognizing those dependencies turns the board from a collection of colors into a small planning problem.

Start by counting distinct colors and estimating how many separate destinations will eventually be needed. In a standard sorting arrangement, the desired end state is one color per completed tube, while any spare tube or open capacity acts as working space during the process. The exact tube count and capacity depend on the level, so avoid assuming every puzzle has the same layout. Instead, ask two practical questions: which colors already have a clear route toward one another, and which colors are blocked by layers above them? The answer helps you choose a first target. A color that appears in several accessible top layers may be easier to consolidate early than a color trapped beneath several unrelated layers.

Read the layers and identify dependencies

A useful scan has three passes. First, look only at the top layer of every tube and identify legal matches. Second, look one or more layers below the surface and notice which colors are preventing access to other colors. Third, inspect empty tubes and partially filled tubes as possible destinations. This sequence reduces visual overload because you are not trying to calculate every future pour at once. It also makes it easier to spot a move that appears legal but has little strategic value. A move is not automatically good because the game accepts it. The better question is whether it creates access, consolidates a color, or preserves a useful space.

Empty tubes are valuable because they provide flexibility, not because an empty tube is inherently a goal. A free tube can temporarily hold a color that blocks another, separate mixed layers, or let you gather matching top segments. Once filled carelessly, that flexibility disappears. Before using an empty tube, decide what job it will perform. If you move a layer into it, can you later move that layer out? Will the destination become a useful staging point, or will it become a container that blocks the only route to a buried color? Thinking of empty tubes as temporary tools rather than permanent parking spaces is one of the strongest habits in liquid sorting games.

When more than one empty tube is available, try to keep their roles distinct. One can be a temporary buffer for uncovering a buried layer; another can remain available for reorganizing a second color group. This is not a strict rule for every board, because some layouts demand that you use all available space. It is a planning preference that protects options for as long as possible. If you fill both spaces with unrelated colors, you may create a position where every visible move is legal but none helps the larger plan. Before committing a buffer, check whether a smaller move could achieve the same result while leaving more capacity unused.

A partially filled tube can be just as useful as an empty one when its top color matches the color you want to move. It can receive a matching layer and help consolidate a color without consuming a completely free tube. However, partial capacity must be checked carefully. A destination may have room for one segment but not for the amount you intend to pour, depending on how the game handles a move. The published rule says the destination needs space; it does not provide a full technical specification for every pour animation. Use the game’s visible legal-move feedback and avoid assuming that a container with any free space can accept every possible transfer.

Use empty tubes as deliberate working space

The most productive moves usually do at least one of three things: join matching colors, uncover a useful layer, or create a better working space. Joining matching colors reduces fragmentation. Uncovering a layer increases the number of future options. Creating working space makes a later sequence possible. A move that does none of these may still be necessary, but it deserves extra scrutiny. For example, shifting a single top segment from one mixed tube to another mixed tube can simply move the obstruction. If the transfer does not reveal a color, bring matching segments together, or improve access, ask whether it is merely rearranging the same problem.

Consolidation is especially helpful when the same color appears at the top of multiple tubes. If a legal destination can receive that color, bringing the matching segments together may reduce the number of places you need to manage. Yet consolidation should not be automatic. Sometimes a top segment is the lid covering a valuable lower layer. Moving it into another tube may create a completed-looking stack that is difficult to move later. Before combining, check what becomes exposed in the source tube and whether the destination will remain useful. The strongest move often improves two parts of the board at once: it gathers a color and reveals the next decision.

A buried color creates a dependency. To reach it, you must first move the layers above it, and those layers need safe destinations. Imagine a tube with yellow above blue above red. If red is needed elsewhere, you cannot reach it until yellow and blue are moved. The planning task is not simply to find a red destination; it is to find a sequence that temporarily stores yellow and blue without blocking the route back. This is where buffer planning matters. Identify the order in which layers must be removed, then check whether the available capacity can support that order. If not, look for another color group that can be completed first to free space.

Do not assume that the deepest visible color should always be your first objective. A different tube may offer an easier consolidation that frees a destination, and that extra capacity can make the buried color accessible later. Work from the board’s constraints rather than from a favorite color. Sometimes the best opening is to finish a nearly complete group. Sometimes it is to move a top layer that blocks two other colors. Sometimes it is to leave a tempting match untouched because it would consume a buffer. The right choice depends on how the entire arrangement is connected.

Choose moves that create progress

A simple planning method is to think one stage ahead, then revise. Choose a move and predict the immediate result: which color becomes visible, how much capacity remains, and which legal matches are created or lost? Then consider one likely follow-up. You do not need to calculate a long chain before every tap. In fact, trying to predict ten moves in a changing puzzle can be slower and less reliable than checking a short sequence and reassessing. The goal is to avoid blind moves while staying responsive to new information. This short-horizon approach is especially useful when a level introduces several mixed tubes or a narrow amount of spare capacity.

Before a move, ask four questions. What will the source tube reveal? What will the destination look like afterward? How much empty capacity will remain? Which next move becomes possible? If you cannot answer at least the first three, pause and inspect the board again. These questions take only a moment once they become familiar. They also provide a practical way to learn from mistakes. If a move later causes a dead end, you can identify whether the problem was a blocked source, a poorly chosen destination, or the loss of a buffer. That is more useful than concluding that the level was simply unlucky.

A deadlock is a position where the remaining legal moves do not appear to advance the puzzle or where the capacity arrangement prevents the sequence you need. Not every temporarily awkward board is truly unsolvable; a useful layer may still be accessible after a different move. To reduce the risk of a deadlock, avoid filling every available space with unrelated colors, avoid splitting a consolidated color without a clear reason, and avoid moving a blocking layer into a tube that will itself become inaccessible. These are warning signs, not mathematical guarantees. A puzzle may require a temporary messy arrangement, but the mess should have a purpose and an exit route.

One common trap is filling a spare tube with a color simply because it is currently on top. The move is legal and visually neat, but the tube becomes unavailable for a later layer that has fewer possible destinations. Another trap is combining two matching segments before checking what the source tube reveals. The combination may be useful, but it can also leave a buried color with no convenient route. A third trap is repeatedly moving a layer between two tubes. If the board returns to almost the same arrangement, the sequence is not creating progress. Stop and reconsider the larger plan rather than continuing the cycle.

Avoid dead ends and recognize unproductive cycles

When you notice a possible cycle, compare the board before and after the sequence. If the same colors remain separated, the same layers are hidden, and no additional capacity has been created, the sequence has not improved the state. Undoing one or more moves can be more efficient than trying to force the arrangement forward. A productive sequence should change something meaningful: a color becomes more consolidated, a lower layer becomes visible, or a buffer becomes available. This test is simple enough to use during play and helps distinguish necessary temporary moves from unproductive shuffling.

Undo is a learning tool as well as a recovery control. The Nextube listing describes an Undo option, which allows you to reconsider a move sequence. When a move creates a worse position, use the control to return to a point where you still had more options. Then try a different destination or order. Avoid treating Undo as a reason to experiment randomly with every possible tap. A better experiment has a question: will moving this layer expose a useful color, or will it consume the only buffer? Testing a specific idea makes the result easier to understand and improves your next decision.

Reset serves a different purpose. Undo is useful when you want to reverse a recent choice while preserving earlier progress. Reset is appropriate when the board has become difficult to reason about or when you want to restart with a clearer plan. The app description lists both controls, but it does not specify every detail of their state behavior or any cost associated with them. Check the in-game interface for the current behavior. If you choose to reset, use what you learned: identify the first move that caused trouble, preserve a buffer for the layer that needs it, and avoid repeating the same sequence without a reason.

A practical way to use Undo is to make a small decision checkpoint. Before a move that consumes a valuable empty tube, note why you are making it. If the next one or two moves do not reveal the expected color or create the expected route, reverse the decision and test another plan. This prevents a long chain of uncertain moves from burying the original mistake. It also makes experimentation less frustrating because each attempt answers a narrow question. Over time, you may find that you need Undo less often, not because you never make mistakes, but because you recognize risky choices earlier.

Use Undo and Reset as planning tools

Difficulty often comes from the number of dependencies rather than from the number of colors alone. A board with several colors can be manageable if matching segments are already exposed and spare capacity is available. A board with fewer colors can be demanding when each target color is buried under a different stack. As levels progress, pay attention to how the arrangement changes: are more layers mixed together, are there fewer convenient buffers, or are the same colors distributed across more tubes? The app describes progression from simpler to more involved planning, but no fixed difficulty formula is published. Treat each board as a new arrangement instead of assuming a particular level number always requires a particular technique.

For a calm session, use a deliberate rhythm: scan, choose, pour, reassess. Rushing can make you overlook a matching top layer or forget that a destination is nearly full. A steady pace is not the same as overthinking. Once the board is familiar, many moves become obvious. Slow down mainly at decision points that use an empty tube, expose a buried color, or split a group that was already consolidated. Those are the choices with the greatest effect on future flexibility. A brief pause at the right moment can save several corrective moves later.

If you enjoy tracking progress, set a goal that is under your control. For example, try to finish a level while keeping one buffer open until the last useful moment, or try to identify the buried color that determines your first major sequence.
